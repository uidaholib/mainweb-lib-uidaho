Over the past decade, gifts to the University of Idaho Library have paid for student fellowships, seeded software used by libraries around the world, helped build three new spaces, and made Idaho's history findable from anywhere. This page is an accounting of what that support has actually produced.

Donor money does something at a library that few other dollars can do: it takes a risk. State and tuition funding keeps the lights on, the databases licensed, and the doors open. Gifts are what let us hire a graduate student to spend a summer on a collection nobody has touched in thirty years, or build a prototype that later attracts a federal grant. Nearly everything below started that way.

Fellowships: paid, mentored research for U of I students

The Library's fellowship program is the clearest example of what donors make possible. It is almost entirely donor funded, and since 2017 it has supported well over ninety fellowships for U of I students and faculty. Fellows are paid a stipend, matched with library faculty mentors, and given a real project with a public result — a digital collection, a processed archive, a map, an application, a published essay.

These are not internships in the fetch-and-carry sense. Fellows produce work that the University and the state keep using long after the fellowship ends.

Gary and Carolyn Strong Special Collections Fellowship

Funds a graduate student each year to increase the visibility and use of previously under-described archival collections. Recent fellows have opened up collections that had sat in the backlog for decades.

Julie and David Levine Data Hub Fellowship

Supports graduate students working alongside library faculty in the Data Hub. Recent Levine Fellows built StoryMaps, web applications, an R Shiny app, and the datasets behind them — then presented their work to the Library Advisory Board.

Lynn and Dennis Baird Fellowship

A newer fellowship focused on wilderness and conservation and the Library's deep archival holdings in those areas. Its first fellow worked on the concept of a "wilderness archive" — research that fed directly into the Taylor Wilderness Research Station Archive.

Berry International Jazz Collections Fellowship

Funds a student each year to raise the profile of the premier jazz archive of the Pacific Northwest, held here at U of I.

Ben J. Plastino Collection Fellowship

Funds a graduate student to write essays contextualizing Plastino's contributions to Idaho's written record.

Dr. Richard W. Wells Fellowship

The Library's newest named fellowship, established through a bequest, supporting the processing and digitization of archival materials.

Alongside the named fellowships, the Library runs its own graduate, undergraduate, faculty development, and metadata fellowships through the Center for Digital Inquiry and Learning. Together they form a pipeline: a student starts with a small metadata fellowship, comes back for a graduate fellowship, and leaves with a public portfolio.

Recent fellows have built Sedimentation: An Agentic Archive of Glen Canyon, enriched the U of I Campus Photograph Collection, opened the Kenneth E. Hungerford Collection, and unpacked what a wilderness archive can be. Every October the Library hosts a public Fellowship Showcase where they present.

Software built in Moscow, Idaho — used around the world

The Center for Digital Inquiry and Learning (CDIL) is the Library's digital scholarship center. Since its founding it has drawn close to $900,000 in competitive external funding across sixteen grants from the Institute of Museum and Library Services, the National Endowment for the Humanities, and the Council on Library and Information Resources — money that came to Idaho because donors and the Library were willing to fund the early, unproven versions first.

CollectionBuilder

An open-source framework for building digital collections and exhibits with minimal infrastructure — no expensive platform, no vendor lock-in. Developed here with IMLS support and now used by libraries, museums, archives, and classrooms worldwide. Its documentation and promotional sites together draw tens of thousands of visits a year from users on every continent.

Oral History as Data

A framework for publishing transcribed oral histories as explorable, visualizable data rather than static PDFs — searchable transcripts, topic views, timelines, downloadable datasets. Built on CollectionBuilder and adopted by oral history projects far beyond Idaho.

Digital Dramaturgy

An NEH-funded collaboration with U of I theatre faculty, creating a publishing framework for dramaturgical research — the deep contextual work behind a production, made shareable and citable.

CDIL has also delivered dozens of digital collections and scholarly projects, many of them built with fellows: the Taylor Wilderness Research Station Archive, Tender Spaces, the Kibbie Dome 50th Anniversary collection, the Political Buttons Collection, and a People's Guide to the Inland Northwest among them. It launched and hosts the first National Interdisciplinary Lookout Conference, which drew more than eighty participants.

And the collections keep finding new readers. Usage of the Library's digital collections grew sharply this past year — total users up roughly 65% and views up about a third over the prior year — while the team reprocessed more than 3,800 PDFs for full-text search and built over 300 audio transcripts to make the material accessible to everyone.

Data, maps, and now AI

The Library's data and GIS program is one of the few in the country where a library — not a research institute — is the principal investigator on major federal grants. Its work has brought in just over $1 million from IMLS for JournalMap, a discovery platform that lets researchers search scientific literature by place rather than keyword, now indexing tens of thousands of articles across more than a thousand journals.

Most recently, the program secured a National Science Foundation award of nearly $700,000 to apply artificial intelligence to historical and current aerial imagery — turning decades of photographs of the Idaho landscape into analyzable data about how that landscape has changed.

Underneath the grants is daily service. The Library manages the University's ArcGIS platforms, which see thousands of unique users and hundreds of thousands of item views each year, and it hosts INSIDE Idaho, the state's geospatial data clearinghouse — a resource used by agencies, consultants, tribes, and citizens across Idaho.

The Data Hub, established in the Library's Map Room, is where that expertise meets people. It is a staffed service point for questions about GIS, data analysis and visualization, research data management, Python and R — and, beginning this year, artificial intelligence. Campus needs help with AI, and those turn out to be information questions. The Library is the natural place to answer them.

Three new spaces in ten years

A library's floor plan is a statement about what it thinks students need. Over the past decade the Library has rebuilt substantial parts of its own — much of it with donor support.

The MILL

Making, Innovating, and Learning Laboratory. Established and then expanded on the first floor, the MILL puts 3D printers, a laser cutter and engraver, and a full complement of making tools in the hands of any student who walks in. It draws roughly 24,000 visits a year, runs a busy workshop series, and partners with programs from Apparel, Textiles & Design to Theatre Arts to Interior Design. Its Mobile MILL takes the equipment to campus events, reaching another thousand-plus people a year.

The Studio

Renovated, relocated, and re-equipped with donor funding — including a WhisperRoom sound-isolation booth, a professional podcasting station, and an editing workstation. The results were immediate: bookings more than doubled year over year, and the space now runs close to fifty hours of use a week. A campus podcasting club started here, and roughly nine in ten bookings come from users who had never used the Studio before.

The Data Hub

Built in the historic Map Room, the Data Hub is both a staffed service point and a working space — classes meet here, research groups use it, workshops run here, and a permanent exhibit on the history of GIS is in development. Use of the room has grown steadily, and a new AI help service launches from it.

The Library also moved its research help desk to the second floor, placing it beside the Writing Center and Vandal Tutoring so that a student who arrives with one kind of question finds all the answers in one place. Study spaces, group rooms, and the first-floor presentation area continue to be among the most heavily booked real estate on campus — the building logs well over a quarter million entries a year.

Special Collections: more found, more used, more visible

Under new leadership, the Library's Special Collections and Archives has dramatically expanded the number of finding aids describing its holdings — the difference between a collection existing and a collection being findable. More than fifteen hundred finding aids are now published, with well over two hundred added or updated every year, and views of them in Archives West have more than doubled in just the last two years.

What that description work makes possible showed up dramatically this past year. When the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act was expanded to cover all of Idaho, Idahoans filing claims needed documentary proof of residency — and they came to the U of I Library. Hundreds of "Downwinder" research requests arrived in a matter of months, and the department became a recognized, trusted resource for Idahoans navigating the federal claims process, in local press and across the state. That is what an archive is for: the dollar value of a well-described collection is rarely so easy to see.

Other growth in the same period:

  • Acquisitions with intent. Dozens of new donations accessioned each year, including major processing projects — the John Osborn papers, the Rachael Osborn papers, and the Sierra Club Northern Rockies Chapter records — that had sat in backlog for decades and now document Idaho's landscapes and wilderness politics.
  • Digital archiving. A digital archivist now guides the acquisition of born-digital collections, and hundreds of gigabytes of University web properties are captured and preserved each year.
  • Teaching. Archival and primary source literacy is taught in classes across campus, plus a one-credit workshop and regular sessions in the Library's information literacy series. The department is working toward historic preservation certification offerings on campus.
  • Statewide presence. Keynotes at community historic preservation events, displays partnering with the Lionel Hampton Jazz Festival and the Smithsonian traveling exhibit Voices and Votes, and dozens of stories a year in the Idaho Harvester and the regional press.

The collections also increasingly live online, through Digital Collections like the Gem of the Mountains Digital Yearbook Collection and the Northwest Historical Postcards Collection, and through the International Jazz Collections.

Teaching, access, and the everyday work

None of the above matters if the ordinary work isn't excellent. It is.

Course reserves that save students real money

In a single recent year, library course reserves saved U of I students over one million dollars in course-materials costs across more than two hundred courses — up from roughly $900,000 the year before. Faculty put required readings on reserve; students don't buy them. It is the least glamorous and most direct affordability program the University runs.

Instruction at scale

Library faculty taught more than two hundred instruction sessions to over 3,500 attendees in a single semester, including intensive partnership with the English composition program — where library instruction and AI literacy are now built into the curriculum and student participation runs above 80%.

The Information Landscape Workshops

Developed for English 102 students and opened to everyone, this workshop series has seen striking demand — dozens of sessions and hundreds of attendees each semester, and growing. Students come because the sessions answer questions they actually have about finding, judging, and using information.

The Renfrew Colloquium

Every Tuesday during the academic year, a U of I researcher presents their work in the Library, free and open to all. Nearly thirty colloquia a year, drawing audiences from across campus and the community — the Taylor Wilderness Archive premiered at one to a crowd of more than eighty.

Behind all of it: a catalog and resource-sharing operation that gets faculty and students what they need from anywhere. Interlibrary loan borrowing rose more than 50% in a year after the Library made it more visible in search; regional Summit borrowing rose 45%. And as the largest library in Idaho, we continue — as we have for over a century — to serve as an official regional depository for U.S. federal government publications, making almost two million government documents available to the public.

Why it works

The pattern in every section above is the same. A donor funds a fellowship, and the fellow's project becomes a digital collection that a federal grant later builds on. A donor funds a renovation, and a room that sat empty becomes a space in use fifty hours a week. A gift pays for a graduate student to describe a collection, and years later that description is what lets an Idahoan document a federal claim.

We will never be the biggest or the richest library. But we are unusually good at turning a modest gift into something that outlasts it — and at making Idaho's story legible to the world.
